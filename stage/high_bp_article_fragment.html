You have your blood pressure checked during a routine doctor’s appointment. A cuff tightens around your arm, the machine displays two numbers, and the nurse tells you your reading.

But what do those numbers actually mean? And how do you know whether they’re something to be concerned about?

Imagine that such an easy test can tell you so much about your health. And tracking it is one of the simplest ways to spot a potential problem before it becomes more serious. That’s especially important because you may feel perfectly fine with no symptoms at all, yet your readings can be high.

Understanding your numbers helps you recognise when something needs attention so you can protect your long-term health.

What Is Blood Pressure?

Every time your heart beats, it pumps blood through vessels called arteries. These carry oxygen and nutrients around your body. As the blood travels, it pushes against the walls of those arteries. This force is called blood pressure.

Some pressure is necessary to keep your blood moving. But when it stays too high for too long, it strains your heart and blood vessels. When this situation happens, you have what is known as high blood pressure or hypertension.

Although hypertension is common, it shouldn’t be ignored. Over time, if it continues uncontrolled, it can increase your risk of heart disease, stroke and kidney problems.

The good news is that this common health problem is often managed through healthy lifestyle changes, medication when needed, and regular monitoring.

What Do Your Blood Pressure Numbers Mean?

A blood pressure reading is written as two numbers, such as 120/80 mmHg, or “120 over 80”. The letters mmHg stand for millimetres of mercury, the unit used to measure blood pressure. Each number measures something different.

The top number (systolic pressure) measures the pressure in your arteries when your heart beats and pushes blood around your body.

The bottom number (diastolic pressure) measures the pressure in your arteries when your heart relaxes between beats.

Both numbers matter because either one can show if your blood pressure is too high.

What Is Considered High Blood Pressure?

A top number of 140 or higher or a bottom number of 90 or higher is considered high. Your doctor usually checks whether either number stays high on two different days before diagnosing high blood pressure.

For example, if your reading is 145/82 on both visits, the top number is high even though the bottom number is below 90. Whereas a top number below 120 and a bottom number below 80 are generally considered normal for most adults.

If you check at home, your doctor may ask you to record your readings over several days. Generally, these readings may be a little lower than those taken at a clinic. This is because people are often more relaxed at home, so their blood pressure may be slightly lower. At any rate, when your doctor reviews the results, they will explain what they mean, and you will know if your blood pressure is too high.

Your age, medical history and other health conditions can influence the range your doctor recommends for you.

Why Is High Blood Pressure a Concern?

If pressure remains high over time, it can damage blood vessels and affect important organs.

This increases your risk of developing conditions such as:

  • Heart disease: High blood pressure makes your heart work harder and can contribute to damage that affects how well it functions
  • Stroke: Damage to blood vessels can increase the risk of a blockage or bleeding in the brain
  • Kidney disease: High blood pressure can damage the small blood vessels in your kidneys, making it harder for them to filter waste from your blood
  • Vision problems: High blood pressure can also damage the blood vessels in your eyes and affect your vision.

These complications don’t happen to everyone with hypertension, and having a high reading doesn’t mean you’ve already developed serious problems.

But keeping your pressure under control can reduce these risks, which is why early detection and regular monitoring matter so much.

Can You Have High Blood Pressure Without Symptoms?

Yes. Most people with high blood pressure don’t have any obvious symptoms.

You can feel energetic, continue your normal activities and have no reason to suspect any problem. It’s why hypertension is often called the silent killer.

Although very high blood pressure can sometimes cause headaches, blurred vision or other symptoms, these aren’t reliable ways to tell whether it is above normal.

Waiting until you feel unwell could mean missing an opportunity to identify and manage the condition earlier.

The only reliable way to know your blood pressure is to have it measured. Regular checks are especially important if this condition runs in your family or if you have diabetes or kidney disease.

How Can You Check Your Blood Pressure at Home?

You don’t always need to visit a doctor’s office to know your numbers.

A suitable home blood pressure monitor can help you check your readings between doctor’s visits, especially if you already have high blood pressure.

Keep in mind that the way you take your reading matters. Even something as simple as talking during the measurement or sitting incorrectly can affect the result.

For more accurate readings, follow these steps:

  1. Choose a suitable monitor. Look for an automatic upper-arm monitor that has been tested and shown to give accurate readings. Make sure the cuff fits your arm properly. Your healthcare professional can help you check whether your device is suitable.
  2. Prepare before measuring. Avoid smoking, exercising or having a drink with caffeine for at least 30 minutes beforehand. Empty your bladder before taking your reading.
  3. Sit comfortably. Sit in a chair with your back against the backrest and your feet flat on the floor. Keep your legs uncrossed. Rest your arm on a table or cushion so that the blood pressure cuff is about the same height as the middle of your chest.
  4. Relax for five minutes. Sit quietly before taking your measurement. Place the cuff around your bare upper arm, and avoid talking or moving while the machine is working.
  5. Record your results. For a routine check, take two readings about one minute apart and write down both results. Your doctor can advise you on how often to check and whether morning and evening readings would be helpful.

Keeping a record allows your doctor to spot changes over time instead of only relying on a single measurement.

The Ministry of Health in Trinidad and Tobago also provides a Know Your Numbers self-management booklet that includes a blood pressure diary for recording readings.

Remember, home monitoring can be helpful, but it doesn’t replace professional medical assessments.

What Should You Do If Your Blood Pressure Is High?

What you do next depends on how high the reading is and whether you have any warning symptoms.

When Is High Blood Pressure an Emergency?

Call 811 for an ambulance immediately if you have severe chest pain or signs of a stroke, such as sudden difficulty speaking or weakness on one side of your body. Don’t wait to take your blood pressure before calling.

If you’re pregnant or have recently given birth, contact your maternity team promptly about a high reading, even if you feel well. You may need care at lower readings than the ones described below.

For other adults, a top number over 180 or a bottom number over 120 is considered high. If you have a reading at this level and especially if you have any of the following symptoms, call 811 immediately:

  • Chest pain
  • Difficulty breathing
  • Sudden weakness or numbness
  • Difficulty speaking
  • Sudden changes in vision
  • Severe, unusual back pain

If you have a reading at this level and no warning symptoms, sit quietly and take another reading after at least one minute. If either number is still that high, contact a healthcare professional straight away.

What If Your Readings Keep Coming Back High?

Blood pressure naturally changes throughout the day. Physical activity, stress, caffeine and even rushing to an appointment can temporarily affect your reading.

If you feel well and your reading is higher than expected but below those very high levels, sit quietly and measure it again using the correct technique. Write down both results.

If your readings stay high, arrange an appointment with your doctor to discuss them. Depending on your circumstances, your doctor may recommend home monitoring or a device that records your blood pressure over 24 hours.

What Can You Do to Keep Your Blood Pressure Under Control?

Whether your blood pressure is already high or you’re hoping to prevent problems in the future, everyday habits can make a meaningful difference.

Some of the most helpful steps include:

  • Reduce your salt intake. Try using less salt when cooking and pay attention to packaged foods, snacks and sauces that may contain more salt (sodium) than you realise
  • Stay physically active. Walking, swimming, cycling and other activities you enjoy can help support healthy blood pressure. Aim for regular movement that suits your health and fitness level
  • Eat a balanced diet. Include plenty of vegetables, fruits, whole grains and other nutritious foods while limiting those that are heavily processed
  • Maintain a healthy weight. If you’re overweight, losing some excess weight may help lower your blood pressure
  • Avoid tobacco and limit alcohol. Both can contribute to heart and blood vessel problems, so making changes in these areas can benefit your overall health
  • Manage stress. Make time to rest, exercise and do things you enjoy. This can help you feel less stressed and make healthier choices.

If you’ve been prescribed blood pressure medication, take it as directed.

Some people generally feel well and after starting treatment they wonder whether they still need their tablets. But a normal reading may simply mean the medication is doing its job.

So never stop taking prescribed blood pressure medication without speaking to your doctor first.

Treatment depends on your health needs, so before making any changes, have a chat with your doctor. Let them help you with any adjustments.

Take a Few Minutes to Know Your Numbers

If you haven’t had your blood pressure checked recently, consider arranging a routine appointment.

You can discuss your results, learn what they mean for you and find out whether any changes or follow-up checks are necessary.
